# Simple vs Compound Interest

**Objective:** Calculate simple and compound interest for a given principal, rate, and time, and explain why compound interest grows faster.

**Estimated time:** 25 minutes

## Learning outcomes

- Calculate simple interest for a given principal, rate, and time.
- Calculate compound interest for a given principal, rate, and time.
- Explain why compound interest grows faster.

## The Magic of Growing Money (~1 min)

Imagine you place $10,000 into a savings account earning a 5 percent annual return [S7]. Over several years, that money will grow, but the exact way it grows depends entirely on how the bank calculates your rewards [S5]. Some accounts add earnings only on your original starting amount, while others reward you on your starting amount plus every dollar of earnings you have accumulated along the way [S5], [S7]. Understanding this distinction is one of the most powerful tools in personal finance [S7], helping you decide how to manage long-term savings [S7] and evaluate the true cost of borrowing money [S4].

## Understanding Interest Concepts, Formulas, and Common Pitfalls (~3 min)

Before calculating financial growth, we must define the core terms. The **principal** is the original amount of money deposited into an account or borrowed on a loan [S3]. The **interest rate** is the percentage used to determine how fast the balance grows over a set period, usually expressed annually and converted into a decimal form for calculations [S3]. **Time** refers to the duration the money is invested or borrowed, typically expressed in years [S3]. Simple and compound interest represent two distinct ways to calculate these earnings [S5]. 

Simple interest is calculated solely on the original principal amount [S5]. Because you always earn rewards on the exact same starting balance, simple interest grows linearly, creating steady and predictable increases over time [S4]. The formula for the final value or total amount including simple interest is expressed as A = P(1 + rt), where P is the principal, r is the annual interest rate as a decimal, and t is the time in years [S5], [S3]. Alternatively, the total interest earned alone can be calculated as I = Prt [S3]. 

Compound interest is calculated on both the original principal and the accumulated interest from previous periods [S4]. This creates a snowball effect often described as earning "interest on interest," where each compounding period adds interest to a growing balance [S4], [S7]. As a result, compound interest grows faster and produces exponential growth over time compared to the linear growth of simple interest [S4]. The formula for compound interest to find the final accumulated amount A is A = P(1 + r)^t, where P is the principal, r is the annual interest rate as a decimal, and t is the time in years [S8]. 

To understand why compound interest grows faster, consider the mechanics of accumulation. With simple interest, your earnings remain constant in each period because the base amount never changes [S8]. For example, if you deposit $10,000 at a 5 percent simple interest rate, your earnings are locked at $500 every single year [S8]. With compound interest, each year's interest is added directly to the principal balance for the next period [S7]. In year two, you earn interest on your initial deposit plus the interest from year one. This accumulation accelerates your growth exponentially over long time horizons, making compound interest vastly superior for long-term investments and retirement savings [S4], [S8]. 

### Common Mistakes to Avoid
When calculating interest, learners often encounter a few recurring pitfalls:
1. **Failing to convert percentages to decimals:** Always divide your percentage rate by 100 before plugging it into formulas (for example, 5% becomes 0.05) [S3].
2. **Confusing time periods:** If a problem specifies months instead of years, you must convert the time into a fraction of a year by dividing the number of months by 12 [S3]. 
3. **Applying compound formulas to simple interest scenarios:** Remember that simple interest ignores accumulated past interest, whereas compound interest depends entirely on it [S5].

## Worked Examples for Simple and Compound Interest (~2 min)

Let us examine four fully worked examples—two for simple interest and two for compound interest—using different numbers to see how the formulas operate in practice.

### Example 1: Simple Interest Calculation (Scenario A)
**Problem:** ₹1,500 is invested for 4 years at 5% per year simple interest. What is the final value of the investment? [S5]
* **Step 1:** State the formula and identify variables: A = P(1 + rt). Here, P = 1500, r = 5% = 0.05, and t = 4 [S5].
* **Step 2:** Substitute the values into the formula: A = 1500 × (1 + (0.05 × 4)) [S5].
* **Step 3:** Perform the arithmetic inside the parentheses: 0.05 × 4 = 0.20. Then 1 + 0.20 = 1.20.
* **Step 4:** Multiply by the principal: A = 1500 × 1.20 = 1,800.
* **Final Answer:** ₹1,800.

### Example 2: Simple Interest Calculation (Scenario B)
**Problem:** $10,000 is invested in a simple interest account with a 2 percent annual rate for 1 year. What is the total balance at the end of the year? [S7]
* **Step 1:** Identify variables: P = 10000, r = 2% = 0.02, t = 1 [S7].
* **Step 2:** Use the simple interest formula for interest earned I = Prt: I = 10000 × 0.02 × 1 = 200 [S7].
* **Step 3:** Add interest to principal to find total value A: 10000 + 200 = 10,200 [S7].
* **Final Answer:** $10,200.

### Example 3: Compound Interest Calculation (Scenario A)
**Problem:** ₹8,500 is invested for 5 years into a bank account at 0.3% per year compound interest. What is the value of the investment after this time? [S5]
* **Step 1:** State the formula and identify variables: A = P(1 + r)^t. Here, P = 8500, r = 0.3% = 0.003, and t = 5 [S5].
* **Step 2:** Substitute into the formula: A = 8500 × (1 + 0.003)^5 [S5].
* **Step 3:** Compute the base: 1 + 0.003 = 1.003.
* **Step 4:** Raise to the power of 5: (1.003)^5 ≈ 1.50896.
* **Step 5:** Multiply by principal: A = 8500 × 1.008985 (approx) = 8,628.79 (rounded to two decimal places).
* **Final Answer:** ₹8,628.79.

### Example 4: Compound Interest Calculation (Scenario B)
**Problem:** $10,000 is deposited into an account earning 5 percent compound interest annually for 3 years. What is the final accumulated amount? [S15]
* **Step 1:** Identify variables: P = 10000, r = 5% = 0.05, t = 3 [S15].
* **Step 2:** Substitute into the compound interest formula: A = 10000 × (1 + 0.05)^3 [S8].
* **Step 3:** Evaluate the expression: (1.05)^3 = 1.157625 [S8].
* **Step 4:** Multiply by principal: A = 10000 × 1.157625 = 11,576.25 [S8].
* **Final Answer:** $11,576.25 (yielding $1,576.25 in total interest compared to $1,500 under simple interest) [S8], [S15].

## Practice Exercises and Applied Explanations (~8 min)

Apply your knowledge by working through the following practice problems. Check your work against the provided model answers.

### Task 1: Calculating Simple Interest
**Problem:** You borrow $4,500 at an annual simple interest rate of 4% for 2 years. What is the total interest accumulated, and what is the total amount owed?
* **Model Answer:** 
  - Convert rate: 4% = 0.04 [S3].
  - Use simple interest formula I = Prt: I = 4500 × 0.04 × 2 = $360 [S3].
  - Total amount owed (Principal + Interest): 4500 + 360 = $4,860.

### Task 2: Calculating Simple Interest with Months
**Problem:** An investment of $2,000 earns simple interest at a rate of 6% per year for 6 months. What is the total interest earned?
* **Model Answer:**
  - Convert time to years: 6 months ÷ 12 = 0.5 years [S3].
  - Convert rate: 6% = 0.06 [S3].
  - Calculate interest: I = 2000 × 0.06 × 0.5 = $60 [S3].

### Task 3: Calculating Compound Interest
**Problem:** You deposit $5,000 into a savings account that earns 4% compound interest annually. How much money will you have in the account after 2 years?
* **Model Answer:**
  - Identify variables: P = 5000, r = 0.04, t = 2 [S8].
  - Use formula A = P(1 + r)^t: A = 5000 × (1 + 0.04)^2 [S8].
  - Calculate: (1.04)^2 = 1.0816 [S8].
  - Multiply by principal: 5000 × 1.0816 = $5,408.

### Task 4: Calculating Compound Interest over Longer Horizons
**Problem:** An account holds $2,000 at an annual compound interest rate of 5% for 3 years. What is the ending balance?
* **Model Answer:**
  - Identify variables: P = 2000, r = 0.05, t = 3 [S8].
  - Formula: A = 2000 × (1 + 0.05)^3 [S8].
  - Calculate: (1.05)^3 = 1.157625 [S8].
  - Multiply by principal: 2000 × 1.157625 = $2,315.25.

### Task 5: Explaining Growth Differences
**Prompt:** In your own words, explain why compound interest grows faster than simple interest over a 10-year period.
* **Model Answer:** Compound interest grows faster because it calculates earnings on both the original principal and all previously accumulated interest from past periods [S4], [S8]. This creates a "snowball effect" or "interest on interest," whereas simple interest calculates earnings solely on the original starting principal every year, resulting in flat, linear growth [S4], [S8].

   - A) $200
   - B) $100
   - C) $400
   - D) $50
   - **Answer:** $100 — Time must be expressed in years, so 6 months is 0.5 years. Multiplying $5,000 × 0.04 × 0.5 gives $100 [S3].

   - A) $11,500.00
   - B) $11,576.25

   - D) $10,500.00
   - **Answer:** $11,576.25 — Using the compound interest formula A = P(1 + r)^t, $10,000 × (1.05)^3 results in $11,576.25 [S8], [S15].

4. An investment of ₹8,500 is placed into an account at 0.3% per year compound interest for 5 years. What is the final value?

   - D) ₹8,600.00
   - **Answer:** ₹8,628.79 — Using A = P(1 + r)^t with P = 8500, r = 0.003, and t = 5 yields approximately ₹8,628.79 [S5].

5. Why does compound interest grow faster than simple interest over extended time periods?
   - A) Compound interest applies a higher interest rate every single month automatically.
   - B) Compound interest earns interest on both the original principal and accumulated interest from prior periods.
   - C) Simple interest decreases the principal balance over time.
   - D) Simple interest does not use time in its calculation.
   - **Answer:** Compound interest earns interest on both the original principal and accumulated interest from prior periods. — Compound interest builds on both the principal and previously earned interest, creating a compounding 'interest on interest' effect that accelerates growth [S4], [S7].

6. What is the primary structural difference in the growth pattern between simple and compound interest over time?
   - A) Simple interest grows exponentially while compound interest grows linearly.
   - B) Simple interest grows linearly with steady increases, while compound interest accelerates into exponential growth.
   - C) Both grow at the exact same rate if the principal and rate are identical.
   - D) Simple interest fluctuates unpredictably while compound interest is constant.
   - **Answer:** Simple interest grows linearly with steady increases, while compound interest accelerates into exponential growth. — Simple interest grows linearly because earnings remain constant on the original principal, whereas compound interest accelerates over time as each period adds interest to the growing principal [S4].

## Lesson Summary (~1 min)

Here are the key takeaways from this lesson:
1. **Principal and Rates:** The principal is your starting amount, and interest rates determine how fast your balance grows when converted to a decimal [S3].
2. **Simple Interest Mechanics:** Simple interest is calculated strictly on the original principal amount using A = P(1 + rt), producing steady, linear growth over time [S4], [S5].
3. **Compound Interest Mechanics:** Compound interest is calculated on both the original principal and accumulated past interest using A = P(1 + r)^t [S4], [S8].
4. **The Power of Compounding:** Compound interest generates exponential growth and higher returns over long periods due to earning "interest on interest" [S4], [S8].
